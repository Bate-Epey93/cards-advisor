# Canadian card rules for a bank cards advisor

Research brief for an intermediate-to-senior customer support curriculum. Compiled 2026-10-12 from primary sources (Justice Laws, FCAC on canada.ca, OBSI, CBA, Visa, Mastercard, Interac, CAFC, OPC). Each entry gives the rule in plain words, the exact figure or wording, the source URL and the source date. Items that could not be verified against a primary source are flagged with **[FLAG]**.

Terminology: "FCPF" is the Financial Consumer Protection Framework (Bank Act Part XII.2 plus the Financial Consumer Protection Framework Regulations, SOR/2021-181), in force since June 30, 2022. "ECB" is the external complaints body. "FRFI" is a federally regulated financial institution.

## 1. Unauthorized transactions

### 1.1 Credit cards: the $50 cap (Bank Act, FCPF)

**Rule.** If someone uses a bank-issued credit card without the client's permission, the most the client can be made to pay is $50, and nothing at all once they have told the bank the card, account information or PIN is lost, stolen or at risk. The bank can only go above $50 if it shows the client was grossly negligent, and the mere fact that the correct PIN or password was used does not prove gross negligence.

**Exact wording (Bank Act s. 627.33).**
- s. 627.33(1): the borrower's maximum liability for unauthorized use of a credit card "is $50, unless the borrower has demonstrated gross negligence or, in Quebec, gross fault" in safeguarding the card, the account information or the personal authentication information.
- s. 627.33(2): after the borrower reports the loss, theft or risk of unauthorized use, the borrower "shall not be liable for any unauthorized use" of the card.
- s. 627.33(3): unauthorized use that occurs through personal authentication information does not, on its own, establish gross negligence or gross fault.

**Source.** Bank Act, S.C. 1991, c. 46, Part XII.2, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/B-1.01/page-68.html (consolidation current to 2026-09-21).

**FCAC consumer wording.** "If a bank issued the card, the maximum amount is $50, unless you demonstrated gross negligence" (gross fault in Quebec). If the issuer is a federally regulated institution other than a bank, liability is the lesser of $50 or the maximum in the card agreement. Gross negligence covers safeguarding "your card and account information, and your personal authentication information, such as your PIN and passwords." The institution "must always fully investigate a transaction that you dispute," and "can't hold you responsible for an unauthorized transaction just because someone used your PIN or another authentication method." Clients should notify the issuer "without delay."
Source: FCAC, "Unauthorized credit and debit transactions: know your rights and responsibilities," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/rights-responsibilities/protection-unauthorized-transactions.html (page date 2025-10-15).

**Older legal basis (still cited in secondary sources).** Before June 30, 2022 the $50 cap sat in s. 12 of the Cost of Borrowing (Banks) Regulations. Anything citing that regulation is describing the pre-FCPF rule; the operative provision today is Bank Act s. 627.33.

**What "not protecting your PIN" means (FCAC).** Sharing it with anyone, "not even a family member"; choosing a PIN "easy to guess like a birth date or a telephone number"; using the same PIN for different accounts and cards. "Shoulder surfing" is someone obtaining access information by looking over your shoulder, for example "while you enter it at an automated teller machine (ATM)." (same FCAC page, 2025-10-15)

### 1.2 FCAC investigation expectation: Bulletin B-6

**Rule.** The bank cannot refuse a claim because "the PIN was used." It must investigate whether something outside the client's control caused the transaction and must be able to show FCAC the steps it took.

**Exact wording.** "FRFIs must investigate whether circumstances beyond the cardholder's control resulted in an unauthorized transaction." Examples: "shoulder-surfing, coercion, card theft, systems malfunction, etc." "A finding of liability cannot solely be based on the fact that a transaction was completed using the correct chip-and-PIN" combination. FCAC "expects FRFIs to report on the specific investigative steps taken before issuing a finding of liability." For banks, the FCPF prevails over the bulletin for conduct after June 30, 2022.
Source: FCAC Bulletin B-6, "Investigations of unauthorized credit and debit card transactions," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/bulletins/investigation-unauthorized-transactions.html (first issued July 2017; page date 2025-10-10). Original news release: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2017/07/fcac_reminds_regulatedinstitutionstothoroughlyinvestigateunautho.html (July 2017).

### 1.3 Interac debit: Canadian Code of Practice for Consumer Debit Card Services

**Status.** Voluntary industry code, first issued May 1992, revised 1996, 2002 and 2004 (with a 2005 investigation-time revision). FCAC monitors compliance. Applies only to debit card plus PIN transactions at ABMs, POS terminals and home terminals in Canada; does not cover transactions outside Canada or cross-border transfers.

**Rule in plain words.** The client is not liable for losses they could not control (system errors, issuer errors, use of a card already reported lost or stolen, cancelled or expired). The client is liable if they "contributed" to the loss: voluntarily disclosing the PIN (including writing it on the card or keeping a poorly disguised written record near the card), choosing a PIN from obvious personal data, or failing to report a lost or stolen card within a reasonable time. Even then, the loss is capped at the card's daily withdrawal or purchase limits (and may exceed the account balance if overdraft or a line of credit is linked). The issuer carries the burden of proof.

**Exact wording.**
- s. 5: "Cardholders are responsible for all authorized use of valid cards." "Cardholders are not liable for losses resulting from circumstances beyond their control," for example "technical problems, card issuer errors and other system malfunctions." Where the cardholder contributed, "This loss will not exceed the established debit card transaction withdrawal limits." Contribution includes "voluntarily disclosing the PIN, including writing the PIN on the card," "keeping a poorly disguised written record of the PIN in proximity with the card," or "failing to notify the issuer, within a reasonable time" that the card was lost, stolen or misused. Disclosure is not voluntary if obtained by coercion, trickery, force or intimidation. Using the same PIN on more than one card "does not constitute contribution to unauthorized use." Choosing a PIN from name, phone number or birth date counts as voluntary disclosure.
- s. 6: the cardholder "should first attempt to resolve the problem with the PIN issuer." The issuer responds "as soon as possible, but in no later than 10 business days"; the limit may be suspended "until such a statement or affidavit is received" if a signed statement or affidavit is requested. s. 6(6): if the cardholder is not fully reimbursed, the PIN issuer must show, on the balance of probabilities, that the cardholder contributed to the unauthorized use.

**Source.** FCAC-hosted text, https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/laws-regulations/debit-card-code-conduct.html (Code "Revised 2004"). FCAC consumer summary: "The maximum amount you're responsible for usually can't be more than the withdrawal limits of your debit card" (FCAC rights page above, 2025-10-15). CBA restatement: "The PIN issuer is responsible for showing that, on the balance of probabilities, the cardholder contributed to the unauthorized use of the card," https://cba.ca/article/debit-card-fraud (undated, CBA).

### 1.4 Visa Zero Liability (Canada)

**Rule.** Visa's network promise: a Canadian cardholder pays nothing for unauthorized Visa transactions, provided they report promptly; the issuer sets the fine print and may exclude ATM and non-Visa PIN transactions and transactions by an authorized user.

**Exact wording.** "Your liability for unauthorized transactions is $0-you pay nothing." Footnote: covers Canada-issued cards; does not apply to anonymous Visa Prepaid, Visa Purchasing, Visa Corporate, Visa Commercial, "or any transactions not processed by Visa"; cardholders "must notify their financial institution immediately of any unauthorized use"; specific restrictions appear "in the issuer's cardholder documentation." Issuers may exclude "a transaction made by a person authorized to transact business on the account" and a transaction by a cardholder "that exceeds the authority given by the account owner." The policy "does not apply to ABM transactions" or PIN transactions not processed by Visa. Provisional credits may be withheld, delayed, limited or reversed for "gross negligence or fraud," delayed reporting, claim verification, and account standing and history.
Source: Visa Canada, "Security," https://visa.ca/en_CA/pay-with-visa/security.html (undated, live 2026-10-12).

**Visa Debit provisional credit.** Visa's Canadian Visa Debit page states the issuer is obligated to provide provisional credit "within two business days" in most circumstances once the disputed transaction has posted. Source: https://www.visa.ca/en/personal/visa-debit-card/index.jsp (undated). **[FLAG]** Captured from a search snippet; page not fetched directly.

**Authorized but disputed charges are not "fraud."** Visa Canada (statement to CBC Marketplace, 2017): where a cardholder expressly authorizes charges by agreeing to terms and conditions and provides their information to a merchant, "it is not fraud, so does not fall under Zero Liability." Source: https://www.cbc.ca/1.4352168 (2017).

### 1.5 Mastercard Zero Liability (Canada)

**Rule.** Same promise, with two explicit conditions: reasonable care (including PIN protection) and immediate reporting.

**Exact wording (issuer copy of Mastercard's terms).** "As a Mastercard cardholder, zero liability applies to your purchases made in the store, over the telephone, online, or via a mobile device and ATM transactions." The issuer "won't hold you responsible for unauthorized use if: 1. you have used reasonable care in safeguarding your card, including not contributing to any unauthorized use, and safeguarding any related Personal Identification Number (PIN) or password; 2. you report the loss or theft of your card immediately after becoming aware of it to your financial institution." "Zero Liability does not apply to Commercial cards*, unregistered prepaid cards or gift cards" (*unless designated "Small Business"). "If applicable law imposes a greater liability or a conflicting obligation, such applicable law shall govern." "Mastercard endorses the Canadian Code of Practice for Consumer Debit Card Services Appendix A. Clause 5 includes long standing guidelines around PIN control, etc. Card issuers may use this in deciding if Zero Liability coverage applies." "Effective October 17, 2014."
Source: Mastercard terms as reproduced by PC Financial, https://www.pcfinancial.ca/api/v2/en-CA/assets/mc_zero_liability.pdf (effective 2014-10-17). Mastercard's own page, https://www.mastercard.ca/en-ca/about-mastercard/what-we-do/terms-of-use/zero-liability-terms-conditions.html, returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch; search-engine snippets of it match the issuer copy word for word.

### 1.6 Putting it together for the advisor

- Credit card: statutory floor is $50 maximum, $0 after report. Network policy makes it $0 in practice if the client reported promptly and took reasonable care. The bank, not the client, must prove gross negligence.
- Debit: $0 for anything beyond the client's control; loss capped at the card's limits if the client contributed; the bank must prove contribution on the balance of probabilities and answer within 10 business days.
- "The PIN was used" is never, on its own, a valid reason to deny (Bank Act s. 627.33(3); FCAC B-6).
- Charges the client authorized (subscriptions, free-trial conversions) are a merchant dispute, not fraud, and are handled under section 2.

## 2. Disputes and chargebacks

### 2.1 How a chargeback works from the cardholder's side

A chargeback is the issuer reversing a card transaction through the network on the cardholder's behalf. Steps: (1) client contacts the issuer; (2) issuer checks reason and timing, and often posts a provisional (temporary) credit; (3) issuer sends the dispute through Visa or Mastercard to the merchant's acquirer; (4) merchant may accept or respond with evidence; (5) if contested, pre-arbitration and then network arbitration; (6) issuer tells the client the outcome and makes the credit permanent or reverses it.

Visa's description: "A cardholder contacts their issuer to question a charge on their billing statement." Participating issuers may open a pre-dispute case; "if a case is resolved, the cardholder receives a refund." "The issuing bank makes a decision, which usually closes the case: to reverse or uphold the disputed chargeback." Visa also says the cardholder "should work directly with the merchant to resolve disputes like cancellations."
Source: Visa, "Chargebacks," https://corporate.visa.com/en/solutions/acceptance/chargebacks.html and "Dispute Management Guidelines for Visa Merchants," June 2024, https://usa.visa.com/dam/VCOM/global/support-legal/documents/merchants-dispute-management-guidelines.pdf.

### 2.2 Time limits

**Visa.** "A cardholder generally has 120 calendar days from the transaction date or 120 calendar days from the last date that the Cardholder expected to receive the merchandise or services, not to exceed 540 days from the transaction date." Source: Visa Dispute Management Guidelines for Visa Merchants, June 2024 (URL above).

**Mastercard.** Cardholder disputes, including fraud and "goods or services not provided," carry a 120-calendar-day limit; some authorization-related reason codes are shorter (90 or 45 days); a 540-day ceiling applies to delayed-delivery cases. **[FLAG]** Mastercard's Chargeback Guide is not freely fetchable; these figures come from an acquirer's official documentation (Adyen) and vendor guides, which agree with each other. Source: Adyen, "Dispute timeframes," https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/understanding-disputes/dispute-timeframes (copyright 2026): "a cardholder has a maximum of 120 calendar days from the transaction date to dispute a transaction"; for Visa and Mastercard delayed-delivery cases "the cardholder has a maximum of 540 calendar days from the transaction date."

**Practical rule for the advisor.** Treat 120 days from the transaction (or from the date the goods or service were due) as the outside limit, and file sooner; issuer agreements often ask clients to report within 30 days of the statement. FCAC: for deposit accounts, "you usually have 30 days after the date of your statement to dispute a transaction," though this varies by institution. Source: FCAC, "Resolving an unauthorized transaction," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/resolving-unauthorized-transaction.html (page date 2025-09-02).

### 2.3 Merchant response and investigation length

Adyen's published network stages (Visa and Mastercard): merchant response to a chargeback, Visa 9 days for US and Canada disputes on or after July 21, 2025 (18 days elsewhere), Mastercard 40 days; issuer-initiated pre-arbitration, Visa 28 days, Mastercard 8 to 28 days by reason code; final decision after the merchant's defence, Visa Collaboration 60 days, Visa Allocation 45 days, Mastercard 70 days; network arbitration "can take several months." Source: https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/understanding-disputes/dispute-timeframes.

**What to tell the client.** A straightforward dispute is usually settled in 30 to 45 days; a contested one can run 60 to 90 days or longer; an arbitrated one can run several months. Give the network stages rather than promising a date. **[FLAG]** There is no Canadian regulatory deadline for credit card chargeback investigations; the only statutory clocks are the 10-business-day Debit Code response (section 1.3) and the 56-day complaint period (section 3) if the client formally complains.

### 2.4 Provisional credit

Visa Canada lists the grounds on which an issuer may withhold, delay, limit or reverse a provisional credit: "gross negligence or fraud," delayed reporting, claim verification, and "account standing and history" (visa.ca security page, section 1.4). Visa Debit: provisional credit in most circumstances "within two business days" of a posted disputed transaction (section 1.4). For credit cards the provisional credit is issuer policy, not law. **[FLAG]** No FCAC or Bank Act requirement to issue provisional credit was found.

### 2.5 Fraud dispute vs merchandise/service dispute

| | Fraud (unauthorized) | Merchandise or service (authorized but wrong) |
|---|---|---|
| Who made the transaction | Not the client, not an authorized user | The client, or someone they authorized |
| Governing rule | Bank Act s. 627.33, Zero Liability, Debit Code, FCAC B-6 | Network chargeback rules (reason codes for not received, not as described, defective, credit not processed, cancelled recurring, duplicate) |
| Liability | $50 max by law, $0 in practice | None; outcome depends on evidence |
| First step | Block the card, report, investigate; no merchant contact needed | Client should try the merchant first; issuer will usually ask for proof of that attempt |
| Evidence | Client statement or affidavit; bank's transaction data; sometimes police report | Order confirmation, receipt, merchant correspondence, return tracking, cancellation confirmation, photos of defect |
| Risk | Repeated fraud claims with gross negligence can reduce protection | Disputing a legitimate charge is "friendly fraud" and can be reversed against the client |

Visa on friendly fraud: "when a cardholder disputes a legitimate transaction that they made or someone in their household made." Source: https://corporate.visa.com/en/solutions/visa-protect/insights/friendly-fraud.html.

**"Merchant dispute first."** Visa's guidelines expect the cardholder to work with the merchant on cancellations and similar disputes; most Canadian issuers require evidence of a merchant contact attempt before opening a non-fraud dispute. **[FLAG]** This is network guidance and issuer practice, not an FCAC rule; no canada.ca page states it. The Debit Code does say the cardholder "should first attempt to resolve the problem with the PIN issuer" (section 1.3), which is a different point.

## 3. Complaint handling

### 3.1 The FCPF complaint rules (Bank Act Part XII.2, in force June 30, 2022)

**Rule in plain words.** Every expression of dissatisfaction is a complaint and must be recorded. The bank must acknowledge it in writing, hand it to a designated employee within 14 days if frontline staff cannot resolve it, resolve or close it within 56 days without stopping the clock, tell the client in writing how it was decided and that they can go to OBSI, and report complaints to FCAC every quarter.

**Exact wording.**
- Bank Act s. 627.43(1): the institution shall "(a) establish procedures that are satisfactory to the Commissioner for dealing, within the prescribed period, with complaints"; "(b) designate one of its officers or employees in Canada to be responsible for implementing those procedures"; "(c) designate one or more of its officers or employees in Canada to receive and deal with those complaints."
- s. 627.43(2): the bank may not use misleading terms, such as "ombudsman," that suggest its internal complaint staff are independent of the bank.
- s. 627.43(4): the bank must give the complainant "(a) a written acknowledgment of the date on which it received the complaint;" (b) the information in s. 627.65(a) to (c); (c) any information needed to follow its procedures.
- s. 627.65: the bank must disclose "its procedures for dealing with complaints established under paragraph 627.43(1)(a);" "the name of the external complaints body and the manner in which that body may be contacted; and" "the Agency's mailing address, website address and telephone number." (The Agency is FCAC.)
- s. 627.44: complaint records kept "for a period of at least seven years."
- s. 627.46: complaint records go to the Commissioner "within 60 days after the end of each quarter."
- s. 627.47: annual public complaint statistics "free of charge and within 135 days after the end of each financial year," including complaints handled by the designated officer who "holds the most senior position" for that purpose.
- s. 627.48(3): "Every institution shall be a member of the external complaints body."
- FCPF Regulations s. 14: the prescribed period for dealing with a complaint "is 56 days after the day on which the complaint is received."

Sources: Bank Act, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/B-1.01/page-68.html, page-69.html and page-70.html (current to 2026-09-21); Financial Consumer Protection Framework Regulations, SOR/2021-181, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2021-181/FullText.html (current to 2026-09-21, last amended 2026-03-12).

### 3.2 FCAC Guideline on Complaint-Handling Procedures for Banks

**Key points (current guideline dated January 27, 2022; revised June 23, 2026; revised version takes effect June 23, 2027).**
- Definition: any expression of dissatisfaction from an actual or potential customer counts; records must be kept "whether or not it is justified."
- 56 days: "a Bank must resolve or close a consumer's complaint within the legislated 56-day period." "Banks may not pause the 56 day period." Delays beyond the bank's control must be documented. "Resolved" means the complainant is satisfied; "Closed" means the bank has dealt with it but the complainant is not satisfied.
- 14 days: complaints not resolved by the first employee must go to a Designated Employee within 14 calendar days of first being communicated to the bank; consumers may ask to escalate sooner; if a non-designated employee asks a Designated Employee for help, the complaint counts as referred and must be reported to FCAC.
- **Senior Designated Employee**: the Designated Employee holding the most senior complaint-handling position (may be the same person responsible for implementing the complaint policies). Must have "the experience, competencies, and authority to handle complaints, including providing redress and remediation." The bank publicly reports each year the number of complaints this person handled.
- Acknowledgement: prompt, confirming receipt, explaining timelines, steps and what to expect next, with ECB contact information.
- **Notice of Final Decision** (written), issued when a complaint is closed by a non-designated employee or resolved or closed by a Designated Employee. It must "clearly state that it concludes the Bank's internal process" and should include: the date the complaint was first communicated; the time the bank took; a statement of facts and timelines; the final decision and how it was reached; how any redress or remediation was determined; and the complainant's right to take the complaint to the ECB, with how and when to contact it.
- Redress: a comprehensive policy for timely redress where the Bank Act requires it, and timely remediation for harm.

Source: FCAC, "Guideline on Complaint-Handling Procedures for Banks," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/commissioner-guidance/complaint-handling-procedures-banks.html; news release 2026-06-23, https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2026/06/financial-consumer-agency-of-canada-publishes-revised-guideline-on-complaint-handling-procedures-for-banks.html.

**FCAC enforcement signal (July 31, 2025).** Thematic review of six small and medium-sized banks over eight months found banks did not treat every expression of dissatisfaction as a complaint, "identified multiple instances" of the 56-day limit being missed (including "freezing of the clock" and day-55 alerts), and quarterly reports missing required information. Commissioner Shereen Benzvy Miller: "Effective and timely complaint handling is a cornerstone of trust in our financial system." Sources: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2025/07/fcac-calls-on-banks-to-improve-handling-of-consumer-complaints.html (2025-07-31); https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/programs/research/fcac-supervisory-highlights-thematic-review-complaint-handling.html (July 2025).

### 3.3 OBSI as the single external complaints body

**Dates.** The Minister of Finance designated OBSI as the single ECB for banking on October 17, 2023, on FCAC's recommendation. OBSI "assumed its responsibilities as Canada's single external complaints body (ECB) for federally regulated banks" on **November 1, 2024**, after a 12-month transition. The six banks that used ADR Chambers Banking Ombuds Office (ADRBO), including RBC, TD and Scotiabank, moved to OBSI; ADRBO finished files still open on that date. **[Correction to the brief's premise]**: OBSI became sole ECB in November 2024, not November 2023; the 2023 date is the designation announcement.
Sources: FCAC, 2023-10-17, https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2023/10/designation-dun-organisme-externe-de-traitement-des-plaintes-unique-pour-le-secteur-bancaire-au-canada.html; FCAC, 2024-11-01, https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2024/10/canadians-now-have-a-single-external-complaints-body-for-banking.html.

**OBSI figures.** "You have 180 calendar days to bring your complaint to us once you have received your final response in writing." A bank complaint may also come to OBSI once "56 days have passed since you first complained to the bank or federal credit union in writing." "We can recommend up to $350,000 for a single complaint." "Our decisions are not binding and a firm is not required to follow our recommendation" (refusals are published). OBSI strives "to complete all our banking investigations within 90 calendar days." Source: OBSI FAQs, https://www.obsi.ca/en/how-we-work/faqs/ (live 2026-10-12). Statutory ECB duties (Bank Act s. 627.49): services "free of charge" in "both official languages"; written reasons for declining a complaint within 30 days; final written recommendation "no later than 120 days after the day on which it has all of the information that it requires."

### 3.4 FCAC's role

FCAC supervises banks and the ECB for compliance, including the right to a fair, timely and accessible complaint process, and uses complaint reports to guide supervision, but "does not resolve individual disputes." Source: FCAC 2024-11-01 release above. Clients may still report to FCAC; banks must give FCAC's mailing address, website and phone number (Bank Act s. 627.65(c)).

### 3.5 What the advisor must do on a call

1. Recognise any expression of dissatisfaction as a complaint and log it, even if resolved on the spot.
2. If it cannot be resolved, confirm the written acknowledgement with the received date goes out and that the file reaches a designated employee within 14 days.
3. Never describe internal escalation staff as an "ombudsman."
4. Tell the client, and make sure the written final response says, that they can go to OBSI within 180 days of that response, and give FCAC's contact details.
5. Never tell a client the 56-day clock is paused; it cannot be.

## 4. Fees and interest

### 4.1 Grace period and minimum payment (Bank Act s. 627.34)

**Rule.** The due date for the minimum payment must be at least 21 days after the billing cycle ends, and if the client pays the full balance by the due date there is no interest on that cycle's purchases. The grace period never applies to cash advances, balance transfers or cash-like transactions.

**Exact wording.** s. 627.34(2): the institution "shall not require a minimum payment" that is due "less than 21 days after the last day of that billing cycle." s. 627.34(3): a payment made on the next business day counts as on time when the due date falls on a non-business day. s. 627.34(4): the institution "shall not impose interest on purchases of goods or services" in a billing cycle if the full outstanding balance is paid on or before the due date.
Source: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/B-1.01/page-68.html (current to 2026-09-21).

**FCAC consumer wording.** "Federally regulated financial institutions must provide a minimum 21-day grace period." "The grace period doesn't apply to cash advances, cash-like transactions and balance transfers." "You'll pay interest from the date you get a cash advance until you pay it back in full." "If you go over your limit, you may have to pay an over-the-limit fee," but federally regulated institutions "can't charge over-the-limit fees in certain situations," such as a temporary merchant hold. Source: FCAC, "How credit cards work," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/credit-card-work.html (page date 2025-10-15).

### 4.2 Minimum payment disclosures (FCPF Regulations)

- s. 59(b) and (c): the application information box must show "the length of the interest-free grace period in days, if any, and the circumstances in which it applies" and the minimum payment amount and how it is determined.
- s. 64(1)(c)(ii): initial disclosure must state "the grace period by the end of which the person must have paid that balance."
- s. 66(1)(e): each statement must include "an estimate of the length of time in months and years that would be required to pay in full the outstanding balance" if only the minimum is paid (s. 66(3): not required where the balance must be paid in full).
- FCAC's model agreement example: minimum payment "the greater of 2% of the outstanding balance ... or $10," interest "calculated on your daily average balance and charged monthly." (Illustrative only; issuers set their own, FCAC notes typical minimums of $10 plus interest and fees, or the higher of $10 or about 3% of the balance.)
Sources: SOR/2021-181 FullText (above); FCAC model credit agreement, https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/bulletins/information-box-examples/credit-agreement-credit-card.html; FCAC, "Paying off your credit card," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/pay-off-credit-card.html.

### 4.3 Interest on cash advances

No grace period; interest runs from the day of the advance to the day it is repaid in full, usually at a higher rate than purchases, plus a cash advance fee. FCAC's example contrasts 19% on purchases with 22% on cash advances. Cash-like transactions (wire transfers, money orders, travellers cheques, casino chips, lottery tickets) are treated the same way. Source: FCAC "How credit cards work" (2025-10-15) and FCAC "Video: Understand cash advances," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/credit-card-video.html.

### 4.4 Allocation of payments above the minimum (Bank Act s. 627.35)

When different rates apply, the excess over the minimum goes either (a) first to the highest-rate balance, then down the rate ladder, or (b) proportionally across balances. Useful on calls about why a cash advance balance "never seems to go down." Source: page-68 above.

### 4.5 Over-limit fees and holds (Bank Act s. 627.36)

The institution "shall not impose a charge on a natural person for exceeding their credit limit" when the excess results from a hold on a personal-use credit card; this does not apply if the person would have exceeded the limit anyway during the hold. Source: page-68 above.

### 4.6 Express consent for credit-limit increases (Bank Act s. 627.32)

No credit limit increase, and no credit card cheques, "without first obtaining the person's express consent to do so"; using the card "does not constitute express consent." Oral consent must be confirmed in writing "without delay" (s. 627.08(2)); consent requests must be "clear, simple and not misleading" (s. 627.09). FCAC CG-5: "evergreen" consent is not express consent. Sources: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/B-1.01/page-67.html and page-66.html; https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/commissioner-guidance/guidance-5.html.

### 4.7 Code of Conduct for the Payment Card Industry in Canada (merchant side, cardholder effects only)

The revised Code took effect October 30, 2024 (remaining elements April 30, 2025). Cardholder-relevant points: merchants may offer "discounts for different payment methods and between different payment card networks" (Policy 8); merchants may accept a network's credit or debit "without being obligated to accept both" (Policy 5); mobile wallet users must have "unrestricted discretion to establish their default payment options" (Policy 11). Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/laws-regulations/credit-debit-code-conduct.html (page date 2025-09-24).

**Credit card surcharges (network rules since October 2022, summarised by FCAC).** Merchants may surcharge credit card transactions "(except in Quebec)," to a maximum of 2.4% and never "higher than your actual cost to accept the credit card." "You must clearly disclose surcharges and fees to cardholders before a transaction is completed." "Cardholders must be able to cancel the transaction without penalty before authorizing payment," and "must also be able to pay with an alternative form of payment, like debit or cash." Source: FCAC, "Merchant surcharges, service and convenience fees, and discounts," https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/merchants/credit-fees-merchant.html (page date 2025-10-20). Advisor use: an undisclosed surcharge is a merchant dispute, not fraud, and the client should contact the merchant and the network.

## 5. Vulnerable clients

### 5.1 Code of Conduct for the Delivery of Banking Services to Seniors (CBA, July 2019)

**Status.** Voluntary CBA code; FCAC monitors compliance. Applies to CBA member banks under the Bank Act. "A senior refers to an individual in Canada who is 60 years of age or older and who is transacting for non-business purposes." "Seniors' privacy, decisions and presumption of capacity must be respected." Banks "will take into account applicable accessibility standards."

**Seniors Champion.** "Banks will designate a member of management to promote seniors' interests," with "sufficient stature, knowledge, and resources"; duties include "providing leadership in the implementation of the Code" and "engaging with seniors, subject matter experts and organizations representing seniors."

**The seven principles (verbatim headings).**
1. "Banks will establish and implement appropriate policies, procedures, and processes to support the Code." These cover "training employees and representatives," "identifying circumstances where seniors may require additional assistance with their banking activities," and "identifying and escalating incidents of suspected financial abuse and fraud of seniors."
2. "Banks will communicate effectively with seniors." Includes accessible formats, consolidated seniors information (including the CBA Commitment on Powers of Attorney and Joint Deposit Accounts), and telling seniors about duplicate statements and read-only accounts where offered.
3. "Banks will provide appropriate training to their employees and representatives who serve seniors." Competency-based, covering "financial abuse, fraud and scams," how to discuss them with seniors, the POA commitment, resources, and "escalation processes."
4. "Banks will make appropriate resources available to client-facing employees and representatives to help them understand matters relevant to seniors' banking needs." Includes trained resource persons on "financial abuse, fraud, scams, incapacity."
5. "Banks will endeavor to mitigate potential financial harm to seniors." "Where banks become aware of the likelihood of financial harm to a senior due to financial abuse, fraud or scams, banks will endeavour to mitigate the potential for financial harm, while respecting clients' privacy, and security and autonomy." Examples: fraud-detection transaction monitoring and "quiet, accessible spaces where private discussions can be held."
6. "Banks will take into account market demographics and the needs of seniors when proceeding with branch closures."
7. "Banks will publicly disclose the steps they have taken to support the principles set out in the Code." Annual report on the bank's website "within 135 days following the end of its financial year," copy to FCAC.

**Effective dates.** Seniors Champion by January 1, 2020; Principles 1, 2 and 4 by January 1, 2021; Principle 3 training scheduled by January 1, 2021 and delivered within the year; first Principle 7 report for the 2020 financial year.
Source: CBA, "Code of Conduct for the Delivery of Banking Services to Seniors," https://cba.ca/Assets/CanadianBankersAssociation/Documents/Articles/About_The_Banking_Sector/vol_seniors_en.pdf (July 2019); CBA statement, https://cba.ca/statement-code-of-conduct-banking-services-seniors.

### 5.2 Financial abuse red flags

**Government of Canada (FPT Seniors Forum).** Warning signs include "withdrawals from their bank account or charges to their credit card that they cannot explain," "overdue bills they thought were paid," pressure to sign documents (a POA, a will, "legal or financial documents that you don't understand"), someone "preventing them from making their own financial decisions or accessing their money," "cancellation of service because the bills are not paid," and a person "handling all of the money" or "treating the older adult like a child." Advice: "review your bank statements every month and contact the bank if you see anything unexpected." Forging a signature or using a bank card without permission "is a crime." Sources: https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/corporate/seniors-forum-federal-provincial-territorial/financial-abuse.html; https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/health-risks-safety/how-identify-elder-abuse.html.

**Front-line indicators (National Seniors Council, 2019).** "Frontline staff are positioned to observe red flags such as unusual withdrawals, abrupt changes in account access, or inconsistent behaviour by individuals acting under a POA." Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/national-seniors-council/programs/publications-reports/2019-what-we-heard-financial-crimes-harms.html.

**CBA.** "A sudden change in living arrangements"; "Abrupt or sudden change of beneficiary"; "Confusion about how money is spent or missing money"; "Things are missing." Source: https://cba.ca/article/information-for-seniors (CBA, 2026 copyright, undated).

**FCAC Commissioner (June 2021).** Financial abuse "is hard to spot and often goes unreported"; abusers are often "people seniors trust, like a relative or caregiver." Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/news/2021/06/statement-by-judith-robertson-commissioner-of-the-financial-consumer-agency-of-canadaworld-elder-abuse-awareness-day.html.

**Legal basis for acting on a red flag.** PIPEDA s. 7(3)(d.3) lets the bank disclose without consent "to a government institution, a part of a government institution or the individual's next of kin or authorized representative" if it "has reasonable grounds to believe that the individual has been, is or may be the victim of financial abuse," the disclosure "is made solely for purposes related to preventing or investigating the abuse," and "it is reasonable to expect that disclosure with the knowledge or consent of the individual would compromise the ability to prevent or investigate the abuse." Source: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/page-1.html (current to 2026-09-21). Advisors escalate; they do not decide to disclose.

### 5.3 Appropriate products (Bank Act s. 627.06; FCAC guideline)

Banks must keep "policies and procedures to ensure that the products or services" offered or sold are appropriate for the person "having regard to their circumstances, including their financial needs." FCAC's guideline expects banks to recognise "that consumers are a diverse group, and that some may have financial vulnerabilities," to run an appropriateness assessment "even if the consumer requested the product or service," and to tell the consumer when a product is assessed as unsuitable. Remuneration must not "interfere with the person's ability to comply" (s. 627.07). Sources: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/B-1.01/page-66.html; https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/industry/commissioner-guidance/guideline-appropriate-products-services-banks.html.

### 5.4 Hardship and collections conduct (Bank Act s. 627.37; FCPF Regulations Schedule)

**Rule.** The bank must not harass a debtor or anyone connected to them, and must follow the prescribed practices.

**Exact wording.** s. 627.37: the institution "shall not communicate or attempt to communicate with the person" by means that constitute harassment, including threatening, profane or intimidating language, undue pressure, or publicising the debt. Schedule to SOR/2021-181 (prescribed debt collection practices):
- Item 1: tell the debtor the debt details and who is collecting and their relationship to the institution.
- Item 2: no contact with family or household "except for the sole purpose of obtaining a debtor's address or telephone number," unless the person guaranteed the debt or "the debtor has given their express consent"; oral consent must be confirmed in writing.
- Item 3: employer contact "solely for the purpose of confirming that the debtor is employed," unless the debtor authorises more in writing.
- Item 4: no contact at the workplace unless there is no home address or number, home calls failed, or the debtor authorised it in writing.
- Item 5: without written consent, no contact on a Sunday "except between the hours of 1:00 p.m. and 5:00 p.m. local time for the person being contacted," none "on any other holiday," and on other days only "between the hours of 7:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m. local time for the person being contacted"; no calls that make the debtor pay charges unless they gave a cellular number.
- Item 6: once the debtor asks by registered mail for written-only contact, or to go through a legal advisor, or notifies that the debt is in dispute and headed to a tribunal or court, the institution must respect that.
- Item 7: no misrepresenting the purpose of a call or giving false or misleading information.
- Item 8: collection charges beyond those allowed under s. 627.3 "are not considered to be a part of the amount owing by the debtor and may not be recovered from the debtor."
- Item 9: no collecting from "any person who is not liable for the debt."
- Item 10: no threatening legal action the institution does not intend to take.
- Item 11: no documents that "falsely purport to originate from any court."
Source: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2021-181/FullText.html (current to 2026-09-21). **[FLAG]** No weekly contact-count limit exists in the federal schedule; the "three times a week" rule is provincial (for example Ontario's Collection and Debt Settlement Services Act) and governs third-party agencies, not the bank itself.

**Hardship.** The FCPF has no dedicated credit card hardship rule. FCAC's hardship expectations are set out for mortgages (Guideline on Existing Consumer Mortgage Loans in Exceptional Circumstances, 2023). For cards, the relevant obligations are s. 627.06 (appropriate products), the Seniors Code Principle 5, and the collections schedule. **[FLAG]** Treat card hardship programs as bank policy, not regulation.

### 5.5 Accessibility obligations

**Accessible Canada Act (S.C. 2019, c. 10).** Purpose: "a Canada without barriers, on or before January 1, 2040" (s. 5). Banks are regulated entities as federal works, undertakings or businesses "within the legislative authority of Parliament" (s. 7(1)(e)). Under the Accessible Canada Regulations, federally regulated private-sector entities with 100 or more employees had to publish their first accessibility plan by June 1, 2023, a feedback process at the same time, annual progress reports (first by June 1, 2024), and an updated plan within 36 months; alternate formats of the plan must be provided within 15 days (print, large print, electronic) or 45 days (braille, audio) of a request. Priority areas include "information and communication technologies," "communications," and "design and delivery of programs and services."
Sources: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/A-0.6/page-1.html (current to 2026-09-21); ESDC, "Guidance on accessibility plans," https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/accessible-canada-regulations-guidance/accessibility-plans.html; ESDC, "Summary of the Accessible Canada Regulations," https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/accessible-canada/regulations-summary-act.html.

**Seniors Code link.** Principle 2 requires staff to know "the accessible formats and delivery channels that are available to seniors" and to provide "information in an accessible format" (section 5.1). **[FLAG]** No Bank Act or FCPF provision specifically addresses disability accommodation in service delivery; the duty to accommodate for clients comes from the Canadian Human Rights Act (not researched here) and the ACA.

## 6. Fraud trends 2025 to 2026 (cards and the calls they drive)

### 6.1 CAFC headline numbers

- **2025:** "over 112,000 fraud reports" and "more than $704 million in reported losses" (2024: $638M, 108,878 reports). CAFC estimates only 5% to 10% of victims report. Top 10 by reports in 2025: identity fraud 8,403; investments 4,409 ($351M); service 3,393 ($19.5M); personal information 3,016; phishing 2,869 (467 victims); extortion 2,767 ($23M); merchandise 2,596 ($11.7M); fraud investigator 2,167 reports, 1,137 victims, $28.3M; job 2,148 ($50.6M); relationship 1,093 ($63.3M). Source: CAFC, "Top 10 frauds in 2025," https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2026/02/top-fraud-2025-fraudes-plus-courantes-eng.htm (modified 2026-02-25).
- **First six months of 2026:** approximately **$351 million** reported lost. Bank investigator fraud approximately **$28.6M** (vs $37.4M in all of 2025); spear phishing approximately $57.7M (vs over $68M in 2025); extortion approximately $25.5M (already above 2025's roughly $23M); recovery fraud approximately $13.7M (vs $25.9M in 2025). Source: CAFC, "Fraud Trends, First 6 Months of 2026," https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/features-vedette/2026/08/fraud-trends-tendances-matiere-fraude-eng.htm (modified 2026-08-25). **[FLAG]** The $28.3M "fraud investigator" figure in the top-10 table and the $37.4M "bank investigator" figure in the 2026 bulletin are different CAFC category cuts for 2025; quote each with its source.

### 6.2 The bank investigator scam (the call pattern advisors will hear about most)

CAFC description: "You get an automated phone call claiming to be your financial institution," law enforcement, a credit bureau or Amazon, reporting fraud on the account. "Fraudsters will request access to your computer or device to continue the 'investigation'," then show a fake transaction. Victims are told "you must provide them with the code you get by text message or email in order to confirm your identity," or that they "have to transfer a large amount of money to protect your account," or "Suspects will tell you that they need to get your debit or credit card from your home as part of the investigation." Caller ID is spoofed; scammers may recite the first digits of the card (the BIN, shared by many cardholders). CAFC rules: "Never provide remote access to your computer"; "Don't share codes received via text message or email with anyone"; "These institutions or law enforcement will never threaten you over the phone"; "Institutions or police will never request you to turn over your bank card." Hang up and call the number on the back of the card from a different phone, or wait 10 minutes before calling out. Source: CAFC, "Bank investigator," https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/b-investigator-enqueteur-eng.htm (modified 2026-01-15). Related: recovery pitch, where fraudsters impersonate the CAFC itself, https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/recovery-recuperation-eng.htm.

### 6.3 Card-not-present and card fraud rates

- **Equifax Canada, Q2 2025:** credit card fraud rate 0.75%, up from 0.44% a year earlier (about 75 fraudulent cards per 10,000 vs 44); application fraud rate 0.56%, lowest since Q3 2022. Source: Canadian Press via Investment Executive, 2025-10-01, https://www.investmentexecutive.com/industry-news/credit-card-fraud-rising-in-canada-application-fraud-falling-equifax/.
- **TransUnion Canada, May 2026:** suspected digital fraud rate for attempted transactions from Canada 4.4% in 2025 (global average 3.8%); "stolen credit card fraud or fraudulent charges" was the most common cause of reported digital fraud loss at 26%; median reported loss CAD 1,301. Source: https://newsroom.transunion.ca/digital-fraud-attempts-from-canada-surpass-global-average-as-canadians-who-said-they-lost-money-to-digital-fraud-in-the-last-year-report-losing-a-median-of-cad-1301/ (May 2026). **[FLAG]** Captured from search snippet; not fetched directly.
- **[FLAG]** No CBA, Payments Canada or CAFC breakdown of card-not-present losses for 2024 or 2025 could be found; the CBA's most recent public card-fraud loss series ends in the 2010s. Do not quote a national CNP dollar figure.

### 6.4 Tap (contactless) fraud and "ghost tapping"

- Canadian police warnings about "tap fraud" describe it as starting with a stolen wallet or purse, often from vehicles, then rapid sub-limit taps before the card is cancelled (Kelowna RCMP via Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/10020145/tap-fraud-online-scams-kelowna/amp). Advisor response: block immediately; every tap after the report is $0 liability (Bank Act s. 627.33(2)).
- "Ghost tapping" (unauthorized NFC charges from a hidden terminal or compromised device) is documented mainly in US and Asian cases; Canadian coverage (Loans Canada, updated 2026-02-12; money.ca) is explanatory, not statistical. **[FLAG]** No CAFC, Interac or Canadian police statistics on tap or ghost-tapping losses for 2025 or 2026 were found; Interac's last public debit-fraud figures are from 2016 (0.003% of $351B network volume fraudulent). Source: https://www.interac.ca/en/content/news/interac-debit-card-fraud-losses-at-their-lowest-level-ever/ (2017).

### 6.5 Interac e-Transfer interception and scams

- Mechanism: the recipient's email is compromised, the fraudster watches for transfer notices and answers the security question or redirects the deposit. Banks describe interception as "a growing trend" (Simplii, https://simplii.com/en/preventing-fraud/e-transfer-scams.html). Interac: "Many email accounts are compromised without the owner knowing it. That means fraudsters have stolen the password, and they can log in and lay in wait for messages that could be redirected."
- Defence: Autodeposit. With it, "your funds are deposited automatically without clicking links or answering security questions." Interac updated its Autodeposit guidance on 2025-05-30 citing "the recent rise in fraud attempts against Canadians." Interac also notes an e-Transfer "cannot be reversed once a recipient has deposited the funds," and advises confirming the recipient's legal name shown at Autodeposit before sending. Sources: https://www.interac.ca/en/content/life/three-reasons-to-set-up-interac-e-transfer-autodeposit-today/ (2025-05-30); https://www.interac.ca/en/payments/personal/protect-your-payments/fraud-tips/ (2026); https://www.interac.ca/en/content/life/email-fraud-how-to-recognize-it-and-how-to-protect-yourself/.
- Scale: Central 1 reports its fraud system stopped 70% of fraudulent e-Transfer attempts across 209 financial institutions in calendar 2025 (62% in Q1 2025). Source: Central 1 annual report, https://annualreport.central1.com/fraud-prevention/ (2026). **[FLAG]** Vendor self-reported; no national count of intercepted e-Transfers exists for 2025 or 2026.
- Survey context: Interac poll (Feb 17 to 20, 2026, n=1,500) found 58% had seen a tariff-themed scam in the previous six months and 79% believe AI lets fraudsters build convincing scams faster. Source: https://www.cp24.com/news/money/2026/03/10/scammers-using-current-events-in-fraud-attempts-interac-survey/ (2026-03-10).

### 6.6 Social-engineering patterns that drive card calls (CAFC categories)

Bank investigator (section 6.2); recovery pitch (fee to recover earlier losses, often impersonating CAFC); spear phishing and business email compromise (payment-redirection, $57.7M in H1 2026); extortion (threat of arrest or deportation); merchandise scams (goods not received, $11.7M in 2025, which surface as chargebacks); identity fraud (8,403 reports in 2025, most victims not liable for the resulting losses, which surface as unrecognised accounts and application fraud). Source: CAFC pages in 6.1.

## 7. Privacy and verification

### 7.1 PIPEDA basics for the call

PIPEDA applies to banks as federal works. Ten fair information principles (Schedule 1). The ones that govern a call:
- Principle 3, Consent: "The knowledge and consent of the individual are required for the collection, use, or disclosure" of personal information, "except where inappropriate."
- Principle 5, Limiting use, disclosure and retention: "Unless the individual consents otherwise or it is required by law," information may be used or disclosed only for the purpose it was collected.
- Principle 7, Safeguards: protection "appropriate to the sensitivity of the information."
- Principle 9, Individual access: "Upon request, an individual must be informed of the existence, use, and disclosure" of their personal information and may challenge its accuracy.
Source: OPC, "PIPEDA fair information principles," https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/p_principle/ (modified 2025-05-29).

### 7.2 Verification before disclosure (OPC findings)

- PIPEDA Case Summary 2002-100: an employee asked only for a last name, the customer's mother confirmed, and the balance was disclosed to the wrong person. Lesson: a caller's self-identification is not verification. https://priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2002/pipeda-2002-100
- Case 2003-155: bank policy upheld that "if its representatives are not completely certain of the caller's identity, they must not provide any confidential information." https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2003/pipeda-2003-155/
- Case 2002-108: a representative discussed a card balance with a cardholder's husband who was not a cardholder; breach, because "the card member be positively identified before any personal information is released." https://priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2002/pipeda-2002-108
- Case 2003-175: a teller printed statements for a cardholder's wife; the Assistant Commissioner found the wife "had no right to this information or in fact any information about the account." https://priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2003/pipeda-2003-175
- Case 2003-181 (ex-husband with paperwork): breach because the employee "failed to properly apply standard bank procedures to verify the validity of the ex-spouse's documentation." https://priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2003/pipeda-2003-181
- Case 2004-278: a bank may require a copy of a POA before acting on a daughter's instruction to cancel her father's card; collecting the POA to confirm authority is a purpose "a reasonable person would likely find appropriate" (s. 5(3)). https://priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2004/pipeda-2004-278

**Rules an advisor can state.**
1. Verify the caller fully before saying anything about the account, including whether an account exists.
2. A spouse, parent or adult child has no right to any account information unless they are a joint holder, a named authorized user (for what the agreement permits), or an attorney under a POA the bank has accepted on file.
3. Marriage is not authority. "She's my wife" is not consent; consent must come from the client, and the bank should record any third-party disclosure and the authority relied on.
4. A document waved on a call is not authority until the bank has verified it under its procedures.
5. One-time passcodes are for the client's own authentication; never ask a client to read one that the bank did not just send, and never accept one relayed by a third party.

### 7.3 Disclosure without consent (PIPEDA s. 7(3))

- To a government institution that requests it with lawful authority for law enforcement, national security or to contact next of kin (s. 7(3)(c.1)).
- On the bank's own initiative to a government institution where it "has reasonable grounds to believe that the information relates to a contravention of the laws of Canada" or a province (s. 7(3)(d)).
- Financial abuse exception (s. 7(3)(d.3)): see section 5.2. The OPC's 2017 guidance warns these exceptions "are not to be applied overly broadly."
Source: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/page-1.html (current to 2026-09-21).

### 7.4 Power of attorney basics

- "A power of attorney is a legal document that you sign to give one person" (or more) "the authority to manage your money and property on your behalf." The attorney manages but does not own the assets. "An enduring or continuing power of attorney is a legal document that lets your attorney continue acting for you" if the grantor becomes mentally incapable. The grantor can change or cancel it "at any time, as long as you are mentally capable," and should tell the bank right away. "Banks, credit unions and other financial institutions may also have their own forms to appoint an attorney"; signing a bank form may make another POA invalid, so clients should have the bank confirm their existing POA can be used for banking. Source: FPT Seniors Forum, "Powers of attorney (for financial matters and property) and joint bank accounts," https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/corporate/seniors-forum-federal-provincial-territorial/power-attorney-financial.html (modified 2025-05-07).
- Joint accounts: "any person named on the joint account is able to withdraw money from the account at any time," even if one person deposited everything; creditors of either holder "could make claims on the money in the account." (same page)
- CBA Commitment on Powers of Attorney and Joint Deposit Accounts (2014): banks make available the risks of POAs and joint accounts (or the FPT brochure), and each bank "sets its own minimum requirements for an account to operate under the authority of a POA" (often the original or notarised copy, ID for the attorney, and a review period). Sources: https://cba.ca/Assets/CanadianBankersAssociation/Documents/Articles/About_The_Banking_Sector/vol_poa_joint_account_en.pdf; https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2014/09/backgrounder-voluntary-commitments-banks-powers-attorney-joint-deposit-accounts.html (2014-09).
- Validity is provincial law (for example Ontario's Substitute Decisions Act; Quebec's protection mandate). **[FLAG]** Provincial rules not researched here; the advisor's job is to route the attorney to the bank's POA intake, not to assess validity.
